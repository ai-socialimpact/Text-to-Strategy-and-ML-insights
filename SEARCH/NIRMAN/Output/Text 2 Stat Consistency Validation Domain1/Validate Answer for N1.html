W+ = 657 and W− = 84 sum to 741 = 38·39/2, as required. The very lopsided rank sums (33 positive versus 5 negative signs) give z = −4.24 and p = 2.2e-5. The p-value comes from a normal approximation with a continuity correction, which is reasonable at n_nz = 38 but is still an approximation under heavy ties. Run #2: Parametric Paired t-Test with Cohen's dz and Analytic t-Interval

The t-test treats the ordinal 1–5 item as interval-scaled and tests whether mean delta (0.92) differs from 0. With SD 1.20 and n = 51, SE ≈ 0.17, t ≈ 5.50 and df = 50. The result agrees with the rank tests and is not driven by outliers, since skewness (−0.35) and excess kurtosis (−0.03) are mild. The domain reason to still prefer Wilcoxon is the discrete, tied structure of Likert deltas (only six distinct values, 13 zeros), not non-normality. The t-based p (1.3e-6) is far larger than the normal-approximation p (about 3.9e-8) because the t distribution with 50 df has heavier tails. Both are far below 0.001, so the conclusion is the same. The analytic CI of 0.92 ± 2.0086 × 0.17 gives [0.58, 1.26]. The critical value 2.0086 is the correct t quantile for df = 50. dz = 0.77 is 'medium', just under the 0.8 'large' cutoff. This is why the dz label ('medium') and the rank-biserial label ('large') differ without any real disagreement. The two metrics use different scales and thresholds.

Run #3: Exact Sign Test and Seeded Sign-Flip Permutation Test

The sign test ignores magnitude and uses only direction: 33 improved versus 5 declined, with the 13 unchanged excluded. For 5 of 38 under a fair-coin null, p = 2 × 2.128e-6 = 4.26e-6. This is the most assumption-light result and is deliberately conservative here, because the 13 'same' participants may include people already at or near the ceiling of the 5-point scale who could not improve. The sign-flip permutation tests (mean and W+) hit their resolution floor of 1/10,001 ≈ 9.999e-5, meaning none of 10,000 flips was as extreme as observed. They show p < ~1e-4 but not the true p, and they should not be compared numerically with the analytic p-values. The 'same' count differs by test. The sign test and Wilcoxon exclude 13 zero deltas, whereas the permutation of the mean keeps all 51. This explains small methodological differences without any contradiction. The key point for a domain reader is that significance holds without any distributional assumption.

Run #4: Seeded Percentile Bootstrap with Outlier-Trimmed and Winsorised Location Estimates

Resampling the 51 deltas (mulberry32 seed 42, B = 10,000) gives a mean CI of [0.59, 1.24] and a bootstrap SE of 0.17. This equals the analytic SE, and that equality is expected when the deltas are close to symmetric with no extreme tails. Trimming 5 values from each tail gives a 10% trimmed mean of 0.98, and the winsorised mean is also 0.98. Both are slightly higher than the plain mean of 0.92. This is because the 5 declines (the lowest values) sit in the trimmed tail while the top tail is thinner, so the few decliners pull the plain mean down a little. The median is +1 with a degenerate bootstrap CI of [1, 1]. This is an artefact of integer deltas piling up at +1 and does not indicate high precision. The robust estimates all stay positive and every CI excludes 0, so no handful of extreme respondents drives the result. The sensitivity table, with mean change 0.90 or 0.98 and dz 0.76 or 0.78, shows that one participant out of 52 hardly moves the estimates.

This run shows where the gain happens on the 1–5 life-satisfaction scale. Domain Nuances & Constraints

1) Single-arm observational design: the schema states there is no control group, so results are Pre-to-Post association or change and never causal proof. Regression to the mean is plausible because the Pre mean (3.02) is mid-scale. The Post measurement is taken at the end of the workshop, so social-desirability, demand effects and short-term enthusiasm can inflate scores. 2) Zero is 'not answered', not low: ID 14045's ls_pre = 0 is a missing-data code in a column that otherwise has zero nulls. Treating it as 0 would create an artificial +5 gain, and dropping it removes a possibly informative non-response, which is why the Pre = 5 / Pre = 1 bounding is sensible. 3) Ceiling effect and ties: on a 1–5 item, participants already at 4–5 cannot improve, which partly explains the 13 'same' pairs. Heavy ties are why the Wilcoxon p-value is a tie-corrected normal approximation and why the median bootstrap CI collapses to [1, 1]. 4) Cohort specificity: the result applies to 51 female, Batch-14, Medical-category (non-Pharmacy) participants at Point 1. It should not be generalised to other batches, to males, to Educational_Stream 'Medical / Public Health' (a different, partially-null column) or to Point 2/3 levels (0.2 / 0.3). 5) Effective-N discipline: the demographics table covers batches 1–17 and the questionnaire covers 7–17. Batch 14 is in both, and all 52 cohort members had both Pre and Post rows. For other batches the cohort could shrink on the join. 6) Effect-size labelling: dz = 0.77 is 'medium' and r = 0.77 is 'large' because of different thresholds, and dz is right at the 0.8 boundary. 7) Small-sample imprecision: n = 51 gives reasonably wide CIs ([0.58, 1.26]), and the very small p-values should not be read as precision about the true effect size. 8) Permutation p-values at the 1e-4 floor are bounds and not estimates. 9) Batch recency: Batch 14 is relatively recent (17 is the most recent), but the censoring concern applies to long-term outcomes such as Current_Status_Of_Work, not to an immediate Pre/Post wellbeing score.

Run 3 – Exact sign test and sign-flip permutation

3a. Exact sign test (fully in SQL)

The recursive CTE builds C(n,k) incrementally: C(n,k+1) = C(n,k)·(n−k)/(k+1). This avoids log-factorials but gives the same result.

sql
WITH RECURSIVE sign_inputs AS (
    SELECT scenario,
           COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE delta <> 0)                                  AS n_nz,
           LEAST(COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE delta > 0),
                 COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE delta < 0))                            AS k_min
    FROM   v_scen_deltas
    GROUP  BY scenario
),
binom AS (
    SELECT scenario, n_nz, k_min, 0 AS k, 1.0::float8 AS c          -- C(n,0) = 1
    FROM   sign_inputs
    UNION ALL
    SELECT scenario, n_nz, k_min, k + 1, c * (n_nz - k) / (k + 1)   -- C(n,k+1)
    FROM   binom
    WHERE  k < k_min
)
SELECT scenario, n_nz, k_min                                  AS k,
       SUM(c)                                                 AS sum_binomials,   -- 584,935 for n = 38, k = 5
       SUM(c) * POWER(0.5, n_nz)                              AS one_sided_tail,  -- 2.128e-6
       LEAST(1, 2 * SUM(c) * POWER(0.5, n_nz))                AS p_sign,          -- 4.256e-6
       CASE WHEN LEAST(1, 2 * SUM(c) * POWER(0.5, n_nz)) < 0.001
            THEN '< 0.001' ELSE ROUND((LEAST(1, 2 * SUM(c) * POWER(0.5, n_nz)))::numeric, 2)::text END AS p_display
FROM   binom
GROUP  BY scenario, n_nz, k_min
ORDER  BY scenario;

The catch: We only looked at people who attended. We have no similar group who skipped the workshop. Scores might have risen anyway, for example because people feel better after exams or as time passes. So we can say satisfaction rose after the workshop, but we cannot say the workshop caused it.
