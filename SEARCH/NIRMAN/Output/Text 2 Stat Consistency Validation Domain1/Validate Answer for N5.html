5-Run Domain Explanations (Schema Context)

Run #1: Paired Parametric Mean Aggregation with Paired t-Test

This run is the baseline and follows the questionnaire's own recipe: split by Workshop_Phase, suffix _pre/_post, inner-merge on Participant_Id within Workshop_Level (a merge, not a groupby), then delta = post − pre. Its funnel of 749 → 627 → 619 → 368 → 352 shows that attrition comes from the questionnaire's partial coverage and from Pre-only respondents, not from duplicates (0 in each phase). Mean Pre 2.86 is below the agreement threshold of 4, and mean Post 3.84 is just under it. The mean delta is +0.98 (SD 1.44), so a typical participant moves about one scale point. The statistics are internally consistent: dz = 0.68 and a 95% CI of [0.83, 1.13] that excludes zero. Because the item is a single 1–5 Likert response, the mean is a convenient summary but the ordinal tests in Runs 2–3 are the safer check.

Run #2: Median & Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Non-Parametric Analysis

A 1–5 agreement item is ordinal, so this run checks the result without assuming interval scaling. Medians shift from 3 to 4 (IQR 2–4 to 3–5) and the median delta is +1, matching the mean. 261 of 352 pairs have a non-zero change, and the 91 ties are dropped under the Wilcoxon convention. W+ = 29,664 versus W− = 4,527 shows that positive changes dominate in both number and size (rank-biserial r = 0.74). The exact sign test on 216 improved vs 45 declined (p ≈ 5.7e-28) reaches the same conclusion. The agreement with Run 1 shows the t-test is not an artefact of treating Likert scores as continuous.

Run #3: Pre×Post Transition Matrix & Frequency-Weighted Reconstruction

The 5×5 matrix rebuilds every headline number from cell counts, so it checks the pairing and cleaning logic. The direct mean delta equals the matrix-weighted mean delta, and the zero non-integer flag confirms the item is clean. The delta distribution is 0: 91, +1: 91, +2: 72, +3: 39, +4: 14, with 45 declines, of which 15 are ≥2 points. The marginals show a population-level shift: level 1 falls from 15.06% to 1.99%, level 4 rises from 23.30% to 36.36%, and level 5 rises from 10.23% to 30.40%. Bowker χ² = 118.88 (df=10) confirms the shift is asymmetric. About 125 participants (35.5%) gained ≥2 points, which maps directly onto the schema's secondary ML target 'life-satisfaction gain ≥ 2'. The large share who started at 1–2 can only rise, which is a floor effect that favours improvement.

Run #4: Outlier-Trimmed / Winsorized Delta with Seeded Bootstrap CI

This run tests whether a few extreme jumps (e.g. 1→5) drive the mean. The 5% trimmed mean (k=17 per tail, 318 pairs kept) is 0.99 and the winsorized mean is 0.99, against a raw 0.98. The winsorizing cutoffs are −1 and +3. The bootstrap 95% CI is [0.82, 1.12] (trimmed [0.84, 1.14]), and every resample had a positive mean. On a bounded 1–5 scale outliers are limited by construction, so a stable trimmed mean is expected. The trimmed Pre and Post means (2.85 and 3.90) are trimmed independently, so their difference (1.05) need not equal the trimmed delta. Run 4 labels the funnel differently: N2 = 1,180 questionnaire rows and N2b = 627 participants, whereas Runs 1–3 call the 627 participants N2. This is a labelling difference over the same data, not a discrepancy. The 1,180 rows are all levels and phases for those 627 participants, and 987 rows remain at Level 0.1.

Run #5: Batch-Stratified Volume-Weighted Delta & Cumulative Distribution Dominance

This run uses Workshop_Batch_Number, the cohort and time axis (higher = more recent), to test whether the gain holds across cohorts. The pairs fall in batches 11–17 (n = 25, 36, 44, 51, 108, 79, 9, summing to 352) and all have a positive mean delta. The volume-weighted mean (0.98) matches the overall mean, while the equal-weighted mean of batch means is 0.83 because the small batches (11, 12, 13, 17) gain less. Batch 15 holds about 31% of pairs and has the largest gain (+1.28), so it pulls the pooled mean up. Heterogeneity is borderline (ANOVA p=0.08; Kruskal-Wallis p=0.05). Batch 17 (n=9, mean +0.67) is too small to interpret. Post stochastically dominates Pre at every scale point. Agreement (≥4) rises from 34% to 67% (McNemar b=24, c=141), and the probability of superiority is 0.74. The remark that batches 7–10 are 'missing' is not a data fault. The schema says the Point 1 Pre→Post pairs are in batches 11–17, which suggests Post responses were captured only from batch 11 onward. That reading is an inference from the schema, not something verified in the data, and it should be confirmed.

Domain Nuances & Constraints

1) Completers only: 619 participants have Pre rows but only 368 have Post rows, so about 40% of Pre respondents (and 251 participants) have no Post. If non-responders differ, for example in lower engagement or lower baseline satisfaction, the +0.98 improvement is biased. The results describe completers, not all female Medical participants. 2) Single-arm observational design: there is no comparison group, so timing, retest effects, regression to the mean and selection into completion cannot be separated from any workshop effect. Report association and change, not causation. 3) Ceiling and floor effects: 53 participants began at level 1 (can only stay or rise) and 107 ended at level 5. Mean deltas therefore understate change at the top and overstate it at the bottom. 4) Batch coverage: the paired data are in batches 11–17, even though demographics covers 1–17 and the questionnaire 7–17, so the results do not generalise to earlier cohorts. Batch 17 (n=9) and batch 11 (n=25) are small and unstable. Batch 15 dominates the pool. 5) Cleaning dependence: 0 means 'not answered' and values above 5 are out of range. The 32 invalid values must be NaN before pairing, or Pre means shrink and deltas inflate. 6) Funnel labelling is inconsistent across runs: N2/N2b mean participants (627) in Runs 1–3 but rows (1,180) and participants in Runs 4–5. The substantive counts (749, 627, 619, 368, 352) are identical. 7) Definition traps: use Educational_Stream_Category == 'Medical' (excludes Pharmacy), never Educational_Stream 'Medical / Public Health'. Gender is upper-case 'FEMALE'. Workshop_Level is numeric and must be compared with a tolerance (0.1 floating-point). Participant_Id, Workshop_Name ('F' parses as boolean) and Workshop_Phase must be strings. 8) Metric_Life_Satisfaction_Global is a single global item, not a composite theme. 5d. Exact McNemar p-value

This uses the same log-factorial technique as the Method 2c sign test, applied to b and c.

sql
WITH disc AS (
    SELECT COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE pre_score >= 4 AND post_score < 4)  AS b,
           COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE pre_score < 4  AND post_score >= 4) AS c
    FROM v_pairs_valid
),
params AS (SELECT b + c AS n, LEAST(b, c) AS k FROM disc),
lf AS (
    SELECT 0 AS i, 0::DOUBLE PRECISION AS lnf
    UNION ALL
    SELECT i, SUM(LN(i)) OVER (ORDER BY i)
    FROM generate_series(1, (SELECT n FROM params)) AS i
)
SELECT p.n, p.k,
       LEAST(1, 2 * SUM(EXP(a.lnf - j_.lnf - nj.lnf + p.n * LN(0.5)))) AS mcnemar_exact_p   -- ≈ 2e-21
FROM   params p
CROSS  JOIN generate_series(0, (SELECT k FROM params)) AS j
JOIN   lf a   ON a.i   = p.n
JOIN   lf j_  ON j_.i  = j
JOIN   lf nj  ON nj.i  = p.n - j
GROUP  BY p.n, p.k;

4. Audience 1: the simple, outside-in explanation

Imagine a workshop for young women studying medicine. Before it starts, each person rates how satisfied they are with their life, from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very). After the workshop they rate it again.

  1. We picked only the women in medical studies and excluded pharmacy students.
  2. We kept only those who attended the first workshop (Point 1).
  3. We kept only those who answered the question both before and after, and gave a real answer. A "0" means "didn't answer", so it was not counted as a score.
  4. That left 352 women.

What happened:

  • Before, the average rating was about 2.9.
  • After, it was about 3.8.
  • That is a gain of about 1 point.
  • Out of every 100 women, about 61 went up, 26 stayed the same and 13 went down.

How sure are we that it's real, and not luck? Very sure. Five different analysts looked at the same data in five different ways and all got the same numbers. The chance that a shift this large is random noise is essentially zero.

The caveat: There was no comparison group of women who skipped the workshop. Also, only about 6 in 10 of the women who filled in the "before" survey also filled in the "after" survey. So we know people's scores rose, but we can't say the workshop alone caused it.

Caveats

  1. Single-arm, observational design. There is no control group. Time, retest effects, regression to the mean (Pre scores started low) and other influences can't be separated from the workshop.
  2. Completer bias. Only 368 of 619 Pre respondents have a Post record. If people with better experiences were more likely to answer Post, the gain is overstated.
  3. Ordinal scale. Means treat the 1–5 item as interval data. The Wilcoxon, sign, Bowker and McNemar tests were included because they avoid that assumption, and they agree.
  4. Batch coverage. The questionnaire covers batches 11–17 only. The data contains no batches 7–10, even though some runs describe a 7–17 range.
  5. Labelling inconsistency (cosmetic). Runs 4–5 call 1,180 questionnaire rows "N2" and 627 participants "N2b". Runs 1–3 call 627 "N2". This doesn't affect the final answer.
