What was the question, and what is the answer?

The question

"Are there different 'types' of transformation among workshop participants, and who belongs to each type?"

Imagine every participant filled in a survey before the workshop (Pre) and after it (Post). For each person we can work out how much their answers changed. Examples are how much more they leaned toward NGO careers, how much their sense of life purpose moved, and how much their expected income at age 30 changed.

The question was whether people fall into a few clear groups, such as:

  • the "purpose-driven shift" people,
  • the "income-expectation drop" people,
  • the "little change" people,

and, if so, who is in each group (gender, age, education, state, batch) and how many later ended up in Social Impact Work.

The planned method was:

  1. Calculate each person's change on 12 survey items.
  2. Squeeze those changes into a few main "directions" (PCA).
  3. Use K-Means, a tool that sorts people into k groups of similar people, with k from 3 to 6.
  4. Check the groups with a silhouette score, which measures how cleanly separated they are.

The short answer

We could not show that distinct types exist. The evidence we have points to change being a continuous mix, not a few neat groups.

We also cannot tell you who belongs to each type, how big each type is, or what share of each type is in Social Impact Work. The steps that were supposed to produce those results (Steps 3, 4 and 5) never returned any numbers. Making them up would be dishonest, so the report doesn't.

  • is clearer than what random data would produce,
  • beats "no groups at all,"
  • stays stable when the data is resampled,
  • has no tiny groups (at least 5% of people each),
  • and isn't just a batch-15 effect.

All three steps returned empty output, so we have:

  • ❌ no verified clusters,
  • ❌ no cluster sizes,
  • ❌ no profiles of who belongs where (gender, age, education, state),
  • ❌ no "Social Impact Work" shares.

Safe to say:

  • "Change looks like a spectrum. Some people shifted a lot on one thing and little on others."
  • "You could describe a person as leaning more toward NGO careers, but that is a loose tendency, not a fixed type."

Avoid:

  • "There are 4 types of participants."
  • "Type X is more likely to end up in social impact work."
  • "The workshop caused these changes."

  • Not causation. Everyone attended the workshop and there is no comparison group, so we can't say the workshop caused any change. Some people may simply have adopted more realistic income expectations over time.
  • Selection. Only 1,001 of 1,450 paired participants had complete answers, so the results may not represent everyone.
  • Batch effects. Batch 15 is nearly 40% of the sample and batch 17 is missing.
  • Recent batches haven't had time. If Social Impact Work shares are reported later, recent batches will look lower partly because they haven't had time to get there.
  • Ranking questions force trade-offs, which creates some artificial links between items.
  • Starting level matters. People who started very high can only go down, so change can partly reflect where they began.
  • Small inconsistencies to check: the item lists differ slightly between planning and results, and the direction used for "LifeCriteriaCauseOfWork" should be rechecked.

What to do next

  1. Re-run the cluster tests so they save results at each stage, then check whether any grouping passes all the tests.
  2. If none passes, report the change as a continuum. Describe it with simple gradients, such as low, middle and high on "social-career commitment," and label these as tendencies.
  3. If one passes, then profile who is in each group and their Social Impact Work share. Show the shares by batch band and note that recent batches are censored.
