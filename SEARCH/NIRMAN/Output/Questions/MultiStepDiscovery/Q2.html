Cause of Work and Social Action: Plain-Language Explanation

1. The question

"Do people who care more about the cause behind their work, when choosing a career, also choose to give more of their working life to social action?"

  • "Cause of Work" is one of several career criteria participants ranked, alongside things like financial security, job security, career growth and work-life balance. Rank 1 means "this matters most to me."
  • "Engagement in social action" is what each participant said they plan to do. The options run from Monetary contribution → Occasionally → Volunteering frequently → Part time → Fulltime as a career.

2. The answer

Yes, they go together, but the link is real and modest, not strong or proven.

People who put Cause of Work higher are consistently more likely to plan a deeper commitment, especially full-time. But plenty of people don't follow the pattern, and the data can't show that one causes the other.

3. The takeaway

Cause of Work is a useful signal of who is likely to go deep into social action. It is not a crystal ball, and it is not yet proven to predict what people actually do over a career.

4. An analogy

Think of height and basketball. Taller people are more likely to play basketball seriously, so height is a useful hint. But many short players are excellent, many tall people never touch a ball, and height alone doesn't tell you who will make the team.

Cause of Work is like height here: a real, repeatable hint, but only one factor among many.

5. The evidence

A. Snapshot: who plans full-time social action?

The average Cause-of-Work rank also differs by plan. People planning full-time average 2.69 (Before) and 1.99 (After). People who only plan a monetary contribution average 4.15 and 3.47. A lower number means they ranked it higher.

B. Change within the same people

We followed 1,289 people who answered both before and after.

  • Average Cause-of-Work rank improved from 3.61 to 2.39, so it became more important to them.
  • Full-time plans rose from 28% to 54%.
  • People whose Cause-of-Work importance went up were more likely to move up the engagement ladder (47% vs 33%).
  • Among the 925 people not already planning full-time, those whose Cause-of-Work importance went up were more likely to switch to full-time (43% vs 32%).

C. The "35%" figure

Each one-step rise in Cause-of-Work importance goes with about 35% higher odds of switching to full-time (odds ratio 1.35, plausible range 1.23–1.48).

  • "Odds" are not the same as "chance." It does not mean "35% more people switch."
  • The plain version is the 43% vs 32% gap above.

D. How strong is it?

The correlation is about 0.16, where 0 is no link and 1 is a perfect link. That is small. Cause of Work explains only a small slice of who goes full-time.

The result held up when we removed people who had no room to move up (already at the top) and when we adjusted for workshop batch. That gives some confidence in the direction.

6. What we can't say yet

LimitPlain meaning
Plans ≠ actionsWe measured what people said they'd do at the workshop, not what they did over their working lives. "Productive lifetime" is not yet tested.
Association ≠ causeThere was no comparison group. We can't say the workshop or a Cause-of-Work mindset made anyone choose social action.
Possible proxyRanking is a zero-sum game: if Cause of Work moves up, something else (money, security) moves down. We couldn't test whether Cause of Work matters on its own or only looks important because money concerns fell. The analyses meant to answer this (Steps 4 and 5) returned no results.
Post-workshop enthusiasmPeople may answer in a way that fits what the workshop encouraged.
Who was includedSome "not yet decided" answers were excluded, and only some participants answered both times.

The 68% confidence reflects a consistent direction across analyses, but missing evidence on independence and long-term outcomes.

7. What a volunteer can do with this

  • Use Cause of Work as one conversation starter, not a label. Someone who ranks it #1 is a promising candidate for deeper involvement, but don't assume it.
  • Don't promise that the workshop changes careers. The before-and-after shift is encouraging but isn't proof of impact.
  • To answer the real "lifetime" question, follow up with participants later. Track what they are doing now and how many years they've spent in social action.

One-sentence summary

Participants who rank "Cause of Work" higher tend to plan deeper, more full-time social action, and rising importance goes with a higher chance of switching to full-time (43% vs 32%). The link is modest, based on stated plans rather than long-term actions, and is not proven to be causal.
