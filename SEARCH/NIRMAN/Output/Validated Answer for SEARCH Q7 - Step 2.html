Everyday Explanation of Each Methodology
RUN #1 ✓ Aligned
Parametric Baseline: Pearson Correlation and OLS on Clipped and Log-Transformed Income

Establishes the standard linear-association baseline between Pre income and drop_amount. It runs on both the raw clipped scale and the log scale. The log scale shows whether a linear signal is driven by the heavy right tail of free-typed income.

Calculated Finding: Pearson r = 0.87 raw and 0.64 on log scale (both p<0.05). OLS drop on pre has slope 0.73 and R² 0.75. Post-on-pre slope is 0.27 (<1, regression to the mean). N=1,313; 62.99% reduced. Evidence is consistent with the claim, with caveats.
RUN #2 ✓ Aligned
Robust Non-Parametric: Spearman Rank Correlation, Mann-Whitney U, and Quartile Medians

Free-typed income is extremely skewed, so rank-based statistics are needed. Spearman, Mann-Whitney U and quartile medians are invariant to monotone transforms and immune to outliers. They test whether the ordering of Pre income tracks the ordering of drops, independent of the parametric baseline.

Calculated Finding: Spearman rho = 0.69. Mann-Whitney z = 14.26 and rank-biserial 0.47 (reducers' median pre 120k vs 60k). Reduction rate rises 33%→63%→77%→88% across quartiles. Median % change rises 0→20→40→67%. Rank evidence supports the claim.
RUN #3 ✓ Aligned
Logistic Regression Classification of Reduction with Multivariable Pre-Phase Controls

Reframes the claim as binary prediction: does Pre income predict whether a reduction occurs? Discrimination is measured by the odds ratio and AUC. The run also tests whether other Pre-phase psychometric and demographic predictors add signal beyond Pre income.

Calculated Finding: Model A: OR 2.71 per SD of log pre income (Wald p<0.001), test AUC 0.743. Model B (N=1,068) adds essentially no signal (AUC difference −0.001). Only batch number is significant (OR 0.68). Base rate of reduction 63%.
RUN #4 ✓ Aligned
Outlier-Filtered Drop-Range and 5% Trimmed Sensitivity Analysis

Clipping piles extreme free-typed values onto the bounds, which can create artificial large drops at the 1,000,000 ceiling. This run applies the alternative rule, dropping values outside 5,000–1,000,000, and additionally trims the top and bottom 5% of pre and delta. It shows whether the association depends on extreme or misentered values.

Calculated Finding: Direction and significance hold under drop-range (N=1,290), trimmed (N=1,125) and clip (N=1,313) handling. Spearman is 0.66–0.69 and Pearson raw is 0.75–0.87. Reduction rate Q1→Q4 is 0.33→0.88 (drop-range) and 0.42→0.85 (trimmed). Magnitude is sensitive to outlier handling.
RUN #5 ~ Minor Variation
Regression-to-the-Mean and Ceiling-Effect Corrected Analysis (Percentage Change, Post-on-Pre Slope, Oldham Correction)

delta = post − pre is mathematically coupled with pre, so a positive pre–drop correlation is expected even with pure measurement noise. This run uses scale-free percentage change, the Post-on-Pre slope, and Oldham's method. Oldham's method correlates pre − post with (pre + post)/2 on z-scores, which removes coupling. The run determines whether any signal remains beyond regression to the mean.

Calculated Finding: Verdict: partially supported. The naive association holds (Pearson pre vs drop 0.865, Spearman pre vs pct_change 0.59, median pct_change 0→0.67 across quartiles). Log-OLS slope is 0.505 (CI 0.47–0.54). Oldham corr(d,m) is 0.31 and the variance ratio is 0.61. The latent-score plus noise Monte Carlo puts r_obs inside the null band.

Domain Overview & Consensus Interpretation

NIRMAN is SEARCH's youth social-leadership program. It records subjective psychometrics, captured Pre and Post each workshop, in prod_nirman_questionnaire. Objective facts about each person sit in prod_nirman_demographics. The question asks whether participants who start the Point-1 workshop (Workshop_Level 0.1) with a higher expected monthly income at age 30 (Financial_Expected_Income_Age_30, free-typed INR) show a larger or more likely fall in that expectation by Post. Lower income expectation is one of the schema's named secondary ML targets for 'transformation toward the social sector', so the question is a legitimate domain target. The pipeline reproduces the schema's own cohort. The schema's ML guidance cites 1,450 matched Pre/Post Point-1 pairs, and all runs obtained exactly 1,450 pairs. Cleaning (0 means not answered, then clip to 5,000–1,000,000) leaves 1,313 usable pairs. Of these, 62.99% lowered their expectation, 22.5% were unchanged and 14.5% raised it. The median fell from 100k to 65k and the mean from 166k to 94k, which is the right-skewed shape expected of free-typed income. The consensus is that Pre level and subsequent drop are strongly and robustly associated (Spearman about 0.69, reduction rate about 33% in Q1 to about 88% in Q4, odds ratio 2.7 per SD of log income, AUC about 0.74). Most of that association is mechanical. drop = pre − post contains pre, and post-on-pre slopes of 0.27 (raw) and about 0.5 (log) show strong regression to the mean and variance compression. Because the data are single-arm observational, the finding is an association, not evidence that the workshop caused participants to lower their expectations.

5-Run Domain Explanations (Schema Context)

Run #1: Parametric Baseline: Pearson Correlation and OLS on Clipped and Log-Transformed Income

This run gives the headline linear baseline, with Pearson r = 0.87 raw and 0.64 on the log scale. The raw value is large because Financial_Expected_Income_Age_30 is free-typed monthly INR with a heavy right tail (mean 166k against median 100k). A few very high expectations, including the 25 values clipped at 1,000,000, dominate both the variance of pre and the size of the drops. The raw OLS slope of 0.73 and the post-on-pre slope of 0.27 are the same fact, since slope(drop on pre) = 1 − slope(post on pre). This is the identity that drop_amount = pre − post contains pre. The log result of r = 0.64 and slope 0.49 (against a post-on-pre log slope of about 0.505) is the more credible effect size. It compares proportional, not rupee, changes and is less affected by the clip and by outliers. A post-on-pre slope below 1 means participants who typed extreme Pre figures typed more moderate Post figures. That fits the domain, because free-typed answers are noisy and the Post survey follows a workshop that includes financial-planning content (Financial_Planning_Sheet_Income items belong to the same theme). The run correctly flags the mechanical coupling. N = 1,313 is smaller than the 1,450 pairs because zero (unanswered) incomes were excluded, which is the schema's 'zero_means_not_answered' rule.

Run #2: Robust Non-Parametric: Spearman Rank Correlation, Mann-Whitney U, and Quartile Medians

Rank methods suit this column. Free-typed incomes pile up on round numbers (50k, 60k, 100k), and 22.5% of pairs are exactly unchanged, so many ties are handled with average ranks. They also neutralise the clipped tail. Spearman rho = 0.69 is similar to the raw Pearson but with a very different meaning, because it shows the ordering of Pre tracks the ordering of drop even without the extreme values. The Mann-Whitney comparison (reducers' median Pre 120k against 60k) says that people who lower their expectations start from higher figures. Its comparison group pools 'unchanged' and 'increased', so it blends two different behaviours. The quartile table shows the gradient: reduction rate 33%, 63%, 77%, 88%, and median drop 0, 20k, 60k, 225k. Q1 (5k–55k) has a median drop of 0 largely because of the 5,000 floor. People already near the minimum plausible income cannot fall much, so part of the gradient is a floor/ceiling artefact that the schema's clip rule creates. The run suggests the rising median percentage change (0% to 67%) separates absolute from relative effects. This is only partly true. pct_change = (pre − post)/pre is capped at 1 − 5,000/pre, so low-pre participants are bounded to small values, and it remains subject to regression to the mean. The quartile 'trend correlation of 1.0' on four points is descriptive, not inferential.

Run #3: Logistic Regression Classification of Reduction with Multivariable Pre-Phase Controls

This run recasts the claim as 'does Pre income predict whether a reduction happens', which matches the schema's explainable-ML guidance. Model A has an odds ratio of 2.71 per SD of log Pre income and test AUC 0.743, which is moderate discrimination and not near-deterministic. The base rate is 63%, so AUC is the right metric. Model B adds only Pre-phase predictors, which respects the schema's leakage rule (no Post columns or deltas as predictors). The predictors are the Life_Criteria ranks (inverted, since lower rank = stronger priority), life satisfaction (values above 5 set to NaN), Career_Plan_Social_Action_Engagement mapped to 1–5, Gender and Age_At_Workshop above 0. They add essentially nothing (test AUC difference −0.001, ORs 0.92–1.06). The one exception is Workshop_Batch_Number (OR 0.68), where later cohorts are less likely to reduce. This was a significant Wald term but did not improve test AUC. A batch effect could reflect different facilitators or curriculum, cohort composition, a different income reference point, or the censoring/maturity differences the schema warns about. The data cannot separate these. Domain caution: N fell to 1,068 after the demographics join and dropNa, and the reduction base rate rose to 65.5%, so Model B is estimated on a slightly different and skewed population from Model A. The comparison is made on matched rows and a single 70/30 split, so it is indicative.

Run #4: Outlier-Filtered Drop-Range and 5% Trimmed Sensitivity Analysis

Free-typed incomes can contain unit slips (for example annual instead of monthly), typos and aspirational extremes. Clipping stacks these on the bounds, which the schema permits as an alternative to dropping. This run tests whether the conclusion depends on that choice. Drop-range (N = 1,290), trimmed (N = 1,125) and clip (N = 1,313) all give positive signs, significance and a rising reduction rate by Pre quartile. Magnitude, however, is sensitive. Raw Pearson falls from 0.85–0.87 to 0.75 under trimming, and the Q4 trimmed-mean drop roughly halves (about 244k to 121k), because the very high expectations that generate the largest rupee drops are removed. Spearman (0.66–0.69) and Mann-Whitney effect sizes (0.40–0.47) are the most stable, confirming that the rank-order relationship is the reliable finding and the rupee magnitude is not. The log correlations of 0.87–0.92 are not comparable with Run 1's 0.64. Run 4 correlates log(pre) with log(drop_amount) on positive-drop participants only. That conditions on the outcome and, because log(pre − post) is dominated by log(pre), is mechanically near-collinear with log(pre). Run 1 correlates log(pre) with the log ratio ln(pre) − ln(post) on all pairs. So the difference reflects both the subset and the definition. Run 4's 'trimmed' sample also trims on delta, which again depends on the outcome.

Run #5: Regression-to-the-Mean and Ceiling-Effect Corrected Analysis (Percentage Change, Post-on-Pre Slope, Oldham Correction)

This run asks whether anything is left after the coupling is removed. The numbers match the other runs: N = 1,313, 827 reducers, Pearson 0.865, log slope 0.505 (95% CI 0.473–0.537) and raw slope 0.269. Its distinct contribution is diagnosing why. Post variance is compressed (var ratio 0.61, SD ratio about 0.5). Participants converge to a narrower, more moderate band of expected income after the workshop, whatever they typed at Pre. Positive Oldham correlations (0.31 Pearson, 0.33 Spearman) reflect that compression and do not demonstrate an independent baseline effect. The Monte Carlo puts the observed correlation inside the pure-noise band (log: 0.64 against [0.607, 0.671]). The raw-scale simulation could not match r_pp, so the log scale should be trusted. The 'equal-variance' RTM formula (expected 0.482 against observed 0.865) is invalid here because the variances are unequal. Run 5 itself says so and does not use it as a criterion. Its 'partially supported' verdict comes from a stricter rule it defined for itself (the claim holds only if the association survives the corrections), whereas the other four runs applied 'supported as an association with caveats'. The disagreement is about labelling, not data. In domain terms, this run adds the nuance that 'higher Pre predicts larger drop' is largely a statement about how noisy, free-typed expectations behave when remeasured, and is not strong evidence of a differential workshop response.

Domain Nuances & Constraints

1) Zero-missing does not mean complete: Financial_Expected_Income_Age_30 is in the zero-missing tier, but 0 means 'not answered'. 269 Level-0.1 zeros had to be set to NaN, and 137 matched pairs were lost. 2) Selection into the pair: 2,157 people have a Level-0.1 Pre row but only 1,452 have a Post row. Results describe completers, and nothing in the schema says how Post non-response is distributed across batches 7–17. 3) Free-typed income: values are monthly INR typed by the participant, with extreme outliers. The 5,000 floor and 1,000,000 ceiling are analyst-imposed, and they create the floor effect (Q1 median drop 0) and the ceiling pile-up. Any rupee-scale magnitude (means, Pearson, OLS slope, Q4 drops) is sensitive to this choice. 4) Mechanical coupling and regression to the mean: drop = pre − post is correlated with pre by construction. The post-on-pre slope below 1 (0.27 raw, about 0.5 log) and compressed post variance explain much of the association. Percentage change reduces the scale problem but does not remove regression to the mean. 5) Outcome definition: reduction = 1 only if delta < 0, so the 22.5% unchanged (many at round-number or floor values) are pooled with the 14.5% increasers as 'not reduced'. 6) Short interval and no controls: Pre and Post both belong to the same Point-1 workshop, so the change is an immediate response and says nothing about later career choices, Point-2/3 follow-up, or Current_Status_Of_Work. The data are single-arm observational with no comparison group, so only association can be reported. A fall in expectation may be due to the workshop's content, anchoring from the financial-planning exercise, social desirability, or re-measurement noise. 7) Batch and time: Workshop_Batch_Number (17 = most recent) is the only Pre-phase covariate with detectable signal (OR 0.68), and it may stand for curriculum, facilitators, or cohort composition. The duplicated Workshop_Batch_Number, Workshop_Name and Participant_Id columns across tables must be cast and disambiguated (Participant_Id as string, Workshop_Name 'F' as string) before the Model B join. 8) Join shrinkage: after the demographics join and dropNa, N = 1,068 and the base rate is 65.5% against 63.0%, so Model A and Model B are not on the same population unless refit on matched rows, which Run 3 did. Age_At_Workshop 0 = unknown must be removed. 9) Scale hygiene: rank items (Life_Criteria_*) must be inverted before being used as 'importance' predictors, and Metric_Life_Satisfaction_Global values above 5 set to NaN. Career_Plan_Social_Action_Engagement has 4 nulls and 'Not yet decided / confused' is NaN. 10) Log-scale figures from Run 1 (0.64) and Run 4 (0.87–0.92) are different estimands and should not be compared directly.

Domain Expert Recommendations
  • Report the verdict in two parts. The claim is supported as a descriptive association in the Point-1 cohort: reduction rate rises from about 33% to about 88% across Pre quartiles, with Spearman about 0.69. It is only partly supported as an independent baseline-dependent effect, because the post-on-pre slope of about 0.27 raw (about 0.5 log), compressed post variance and the Monte Carlo band show most of the pattern is regression to the mean and coupling. This reconciles Run 5's 'partially supported' with the other runs' 'supported with caveats'.
  • Make the log scale and rank statistics (Spearman, Mann-Whitney, odds ratio per SD of log income, AUC about 0.74) the primary effect sizes. Treat raw-rupee Pearson and mean drops as outlier-sensitive and show them with the drop-range and trimmed sensitivity figures from Run 4.
  • Break the coupling with an independent baseline measure. Financial_Expected_Income_Age_25 and Financial_Planning_Sheet_Income are in the same finance_expectations theme. Using them as a second Pre-phase predictor (or a reliability check) would separate true baseline-dependent change from remeasurement noise on Age_30.
  • Analyse Post-adjusted-for-Pre (an ANCOVA-type model of log post on log pre plus Workshop_Batch_Number), and consider a three-way outcome (reduced, unchanged, increased). This avoids pooling the unchanged and increased groups and removes the delta-versus-pre correlation by construction.
  • Quantify attrition before generalising. Compare Pre income for the 2,157 Pre respondents against the 1,450 matched pairs and the 1,313 clean pairs, by batch. Report effective N at every step, and use this to state the population (completers of both Point-1 phases with a valid age-30 income answer).
  • Probe the batch effect (OR 0.68) with a batch-wise breakdown of reduction rate and Pre median income across batches 7–17. Check whether later cohorts started lower, changed less, or experienced a different curriculum, rather than interpreting batch as a causal driver.
  • Treat extreme income entries explicitly. Review the 26 clipped and about 23 dropped values for unit errors (monthly versus annual) and document a single cleaning policy. If dropping values, report that clipping would add about 23 pairs, so downstream users can reproduce N.
  • Avoid extending the finding to long-term outcomes. Theoretical Framework & Statistical Invariance

One-sentence answer. The factor that predicts a reduction in expected monthly income at age 30 is the participant's own Pre-workshop value of the same question, with workshop batch number as the only other significant factor and the remaining Pre-phase measures adding nothing.

Supporting evidence. (1) Pre income: among 1,313 clipped pairs, 62.99% reduced. Spearman rho is 0.66–0.69, raw Pearson is 0.75–0.87, and the Mann-Whitney rank-biserial is 0.40–0.47 (median Pre 120k for reducers vs 60k for non-reducers). Reduction rates by Pre-income quartile are 33%→63%→77%→88%. Logistic regression gives OR 2.71 per SD of log Pre income (p<0.001) and test AUC 0.743. (2) Other predictors: in Run 3's multivariable model (N=1,068), LifeCriteriaFinancialSecurity, LifeCriteriaCauseOfWork, CareerPlanSocialActionEngagement, MetricLifeSatisfactionGlobal, Gender and AgeAtWorkshop add no signal (AUC difference −0.001). Only WorkshopBatchNumber is significant (OR 0.68; later batches are less likely to reduce). The runs do not explain this, so it should be read as a cohort or timing difference.

Caveats. The post-on-pre slope is 0.27 raw and about 0.5 on the log scale (CI 0.47–0.54), which indicates regression to the mean. Drop = pre − post is correlated with pre by construction. Run 5 reports an Oldham correlation of 0.31, a variance ratio of 0.61, and a Monte Carlo null band that contains the observed correlation. The percentage-change gradient remains positive (Spearman 0.59). Trimming lowers Pearson to about 0.75 and shifts the quartile reduction rates to 0.42→0.85, so magnitude depends on outlier handling.

Verdict. Four runs call the claim supported and Run 5 calls it partially supported, with matching numbers. This is an association only, since the data are single-arm and observational.
