Domain Overview & Consensus Interpretation

The question asks whether women and men in NIRMAN's Point-1 workshop (the first-stage workshop, Workshop_Level 0.1 in prod_nirman_questionnaire) differ in two Pre→Post shifts. The first is moving into the 'Fulltime as a career' category on Career_Plan_Social_Action_Engagement, which is the schema's primary questionnaire outcome (switched_to_fulltime). The second is the change in Metric_Life_Satisfaction_Global, a 1–5 agreement item. The questionnaire holds one row per (Participant_Id, Workshop_Level, Workshop_Phase). The matched-pair recipe (split Pre/Post, suffix, inner-merge on string Participant_Id) therefore turns it into one row per participant. Gender comes from prod_nirman_demographics, a one-row-per-participant registry, through a left join on string Participant_Id. All five runs agree on the pipeline: 1,450 matched pairs (777 FEMALE, 673 MALE), with no loss at the Gender join or the career-plan null drop. This fits the YAML, where Gender is a zero-missing usable column with only FEMALE/MALE values. Among the 1,085 participants not full-time at Pre (573 F, 512 M), 42.58% of women and 36.52% of men reported full-time at Post, a gap of +6.06 pp that is borderline (p≈0.04–0.06). Life satisfaction rose about one scale point in both genders (F 1.12, M 1.00 on n=703 F and 604 M). The +0.12 difference is not significant (p≈0.10–0.13). All of this is single-arm observational association. The data contain no control group, no workshop-assignment variable and no causal identification, so none of it can be read as a workshop effect.

5-Run Domain Explanations (Schema Context)

Run #1: Direct Proportion & Mean Aggregation with Two-Proportion Z-Test and Welch's t-Test

This run is the reference calculation. It applies the schema recipe literally: switched_to_fulltime = is_fulltime_post==1 restricted to is_fulltime_pre==0, and delta = post − pre. Women convert 244/573 (42.58%) against men's 187/512 (36.52%), a 6.06 pp gap. The pooled z-test (z=2.04, p=0.04) and the Wald CI (0.25–11.87) show the gap only just excludes zero. The life-satisfaction result is a roughly one-point rise on the 1–5 item in both genders, with a +0.12 gap (Welch t=1.53, df≈1301.7, p=0.13, d=0.08). Domain meaning: after the Point-1 workshop, both genders report much higher satisfaction, and the difference between them is practically negligible. The denominator includes participants whose Pre answer was 'Not yet decided / confused', which counts as is_fulltime=0 under the requested definition. The parametric tests assume approximately normal sampling distributions, which is reasonable with n≈500–700 per group.

Run #2: Robust Non-Parametric: Wilson Intervals, Fisher Exact Test, Medians/IQR and Mann-Whitney U

The 1–5 agreement scale produces a discrete delta, mostly −1 to +3, with many ties and a median of 1 in both genders, so rank-based tests suit it. Counts and rates are identical to Run 1. Fisher and Yates chi-square (p≈0.05) are slightly more conservative than the uncorrected z-test (p=0.04), which is why the same gap flips from 'significant' to 'borderline'. The Newcombe CI (0.23–11.82) matches the Wald CI closely. The post-workshop plan distribution among the at-risk group is dominated by 'Part time' (about 33%) and 'Volunteering frequently' (19–22%), so full-time is the top rung of a graded engagement ladder. For life satisfaction, 66.86% of women and 65.23% of men improved, 22.76% and 25.00% were unchanged, and 10.38% and 9.77% declined. Mann-Whitney finds no significant gender gap in change (p=0.10, rank-biserial about 0.05). Within-gender Wilcoxon tests (p<0.001) show a strong Pre→Post shift in each group. That is a large within-person change, not a between-gender difference. It also shows 'significant' here describes association within a single-arm cohort, with no counterfactual.

Run #3: Batch-Stratified Volume-Weighted Estimation (Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel & Inverse-Variance Pooling)

The schema warns that later batches have had less time to realise outcomes (censoring) and that Workshop_Batch_Number is a cohort id (higher = more recent). Stratifying by batch tests whether the gender gap is a cohort-composition artefact. It is not: the MH risk difference (5.68 pp), the direct-standardised gap (42.28% vs 36.60%) and OR_MH of 1.27 are all close to the crude 6.06 pp. The batch-adjusted CMH p is 0.06, borderline like the others. Batch heterogeneity is large, though: batch 14 shows +23.57 pp while batches 11 (−6.75) and 17 (−8.59) lean toward men. With small strata these swings are unstable, so a single pooled gender gap hides cohort-specific facilitation, recruitment or measurement differences. For life satisfaction the IV-pooled gap is 0.12 (CI −0.03 to 0.26, p=0.11). Batch 17 loses most of its data after cleaning (174 → 35 pairs). That suggests many zero-coded 'not answered' responses for this item in the most recent cohort, and the inverse-variance weights rightly discount it. Matched pairs occur only in batches 11–17, so earlier questionnaire batches (7–10) contribute nothing to this comparison.

Run #4: Outlier-Trimmed Means with Bootstrap Resampling Confidence Intervals

Possible extreme swings (±4) on a bounded 1–5 item might distort the mean delta. The 5% trimming (35 values per end for women, 30 for men) leaves the gap unchanged (1.12 vs 1.00, gap 0.12), so no outlier-driven artefact exists. This is plausible for a bounded item with a median shift of 1 in both groups. Yuen's test (p=0.10) and Welch's (p=0.13) agree. Bootstrap CIs for the mean-delta gap (−0.04 to 0.26) and the trimmed gap (−0.03 to 0.27) include zero. The bootstrap CI for the switch-rate gap (0.31–12.38) just excludes zero, which matches the Wald and Newcombe intervals. Resampling participants within gender also respects the one-row-per-participant grain after the Pre/Post merge. Both outcomes are therefore stable to outliers and sampling variability, and the only fragility is the switch-rate gap sitting near the p≈0.05 boundary.

Run #5: Rank-Order Ordinal Transition Matrix & Cumulative Share Analysis

This run unpacks the 1–5 ordinal ladder (Monetary contribution=1 … Fulltime as a career=5, plus 'Not yet decided / confused' as a separate state). It locates where the gender gap arises. Among pairs with both plans in 1–5, directional movement is almost identical: moving up 40.69% (F) vs 41.37% (M), staying 53.82% vs 53.27%, moving down 5.48% vs 5.36%. The female edge therefore comes from conversion into the top category, not from a generally more upward trajectory. By Pre category, women convert more from Part time (47.58% vs 43.78%), Volunteering (34.68% vs 30.77%) and especially undecided (54.43% vs 42.67%). The undecided stratum is the largest gap, about 12 pp, and is a definitional choice since the schema treats undecided as null in ordinal models while the question counts it as not full-time. Pre=1 and 2 strata are tiny (n=7 F and 12 M for Pre=2), so those rates are unstable. For life satisfaction, women have a higher share of gains ≥2 points (37.98% vs 32.45%) but similar ≥1 shares (66.86% vs 65.23%). Cliff's delta of 0.05 is negligible and Mann-Whitney p=0.10, and the cumulative curves cross at the low end, so neither gender strictly dominates. Pearson chi-square (4.15, p=0.04) vs Yates (3.90, p=0.05) again shows the borderline nature of the switch-rate gap.

Domain Nuances & Constraints

1) Single-arm observational data: the schema is explicit that only association and change can be reported. There is no unexposed comparison group, so Pre→Post gains can reflect the workshop, survey demand effects (Post is collected immediately after), regression toward the mean (Pre life satisfaction averages about 2.9), maturation or self-report bias. 2) Selection into the matched sample: 2,157 Pre vs 1,452 Post rows at Point 1 means about a third of Pre respondents have no Post record. Those with both phases are not a random subset, and the join to demographics and the batch 11–17 restriction change N, so effective N must always be quoted. 3) Part (a) and part (b) use different subsets (1,085 'at risk' vs 1,307 cleaned pairs), so results should not be read as one cohort. 4) Zero-coded life-satisfaction responses (142 Pre, 141 Post) are 'not answered', not low scores. They are concentrated in recent batches (batch 17 drops from 174 to 35 pairs), so dropping them may not be random and the batch-17 life-satisfaction estimate is very imprecise. 5) 'Fulltime as a career' is a stated plan or intention for social action, not realised employment. It is a different construct from demographics.Current_Status_Of_Work, which is an undated snapshot of present work. Starting-state dependence matters too: the 365 already full-time at Pre (204 F, 161 M) are excluded by design, and the 'Not yet decided / confused' state is counted as non-full-time here although the schema treats it as null in ordinal models. 6) The switch-rate gap of 6.06 pp sits at p≈0.04–0.06 across tests, so its 'significance' depends on the test (pooled z and Pearson give 0.04; Yates, Fisher and CMH give 0.05–0.06). Across the many tests run, no multiplicity adjustment was applied, and the gap should be called suggestive. 7) Batch heterogeneity (batch 14 +23.57 pp vs batches 11 and 17 negative) suggests cohort-specific effects, such as facilitators, venues or timing, that a pooled gender gap hides. The recent-batch censoring rule applies to long-term outcomes, less so to immediate Pre/Post intentions. 8) Gender is binary (FEMALE/MALE) in the registry, so the comparison cannot speak to other identities. Age, education stream and native state are unadjusted confounders. 9) Life satisfaction is a bounded 1–5 item with ceiling effects, since high-Pre respondents cannot gain much. The gender gap in mean delta (0.12, d≈0.08, Cliff's 0.05) is practically negligible even where nominally borderline.

Theoretical Framework & Statistical Invariance

Plain-language overview: all five runs, using different statistical approaches, produced the same core results. They differ only in how they judged whether the gender gaps could be due to chance.

  • Batch check: adjusting for workshop batch moves the gap only slightly, to 5.68 pp, so batch mix does not explain it. The gap is uneven across batches: batch 14 shows a large positive gap, while batches 11 and 17 lean slightly negative.
  • Life satisfaction: women improved from 2.86 to 3.98 (+1.12) and men from 2.95 to 3.95 (+1.00). The effect size is negligible (Cohen's d=0.08, Cliff's delta=0.05).
  • Robustness: trimmed and untrimmed means agree and both medians are 1, so outliers are not driving the result. Women do have a slightly larger share with a gain of 2 or more points (37.98% vs 32.45%), but the overall difference remains small.
  • Within each gender, improvement from Pre to Post is clearly different from zero (Wilcoxon p<0.001). This describes change over time, not a workshop effect, since there is no control group.
  • Caveats: parts (a) and (b) use different subsets of people. Batch 17 loses many life-satisfaction records after cleaning. Participants are self-selected and there is no comparison group, so all findings are associations only.
