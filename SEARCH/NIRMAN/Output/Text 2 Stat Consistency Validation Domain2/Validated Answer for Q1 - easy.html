4 4. Answer Contextualized by Schema & Domain Model
How the Project Schema Explains & Validates This Answer:

The question asks whether women and men in NIRMAN's Point-1 workshop (the first-stage workshop, Workshop_Level 0.1 in prod_nirman_questionnaire) differ in two Pre→Post shifts. The first is moving into the 'Fulltime as a career' category on Career_Plan_Social_Action_Engagement, which is the schema's primary questionnaire outcome (switched_to_fulltime). The second is the change in Metric_Life_Satisfaction_Global, a 1–5 agreement item. The questionnaire holds one row per (Participant_Id, Workshop_Level, Workshop_Phase). The matched-pair recipe (split Pre/Post, suffix, inner-merge on string Participant_Id) therefore turns it into one row per participant. Gender comes from prod_nirman_demographics, a one-row-per-participant registry, through a left join on string Participant_Id. All five runs agree on the pipeline: 1,450 matched pairs (777 FEMALE, 673 MALE), with no loss at the Gender join or the career-plan null drop. This fits the YAML, where Gender is a zero-missing usable column with only FEMALE/MALE values. Among the 1,085 participants not full-time at Pre (573 F, 512 M), 42.58% of women and 36.52% of men reported full-time at Post, a gap of +6.06 pp that is borderline (p≈0.04–0.06). Life satisfaction rose about one scale point in both genders (F 1.12, M 1.00 on n=703 F and 604 M). The +0.12 difference is not significant (p≈0.10–0.13). All of this is single-arm observational association. The data contain no control group, no workshop-assignment variable and no causal identification, so none of it can be read as a workshop effect.

Domain Business Insights from Schema:
  • Report the headline as: among matched Point-1 pairs (N=1,450; 777 F, 673 M), women were somewhat more likely than men to report 'Fulltime as a career' at Post if not already full-time at Pre (42.58% vs 36.52%, +6.06 pp, borderline), while life-satisfaction gains were similar (+1.12 vs +1.00). Do not describe this as a workshop effect or a gender causal effect.
  • Always cite effective N at each stage (1,450 matched; 1,085 at risk with 573 F and 512 M; 1,307 valid life-satisfaction pairs with 703 F and 604 M). Keep parts (a) and (b) clearly labelled as different subsets.
  • Treat the switch-rate gender gap as a hypothesis for confirmation, not a finding. Test it on a holdout of future batches or add covariates (Age_At_Workshop > 0, Educational_Stream_Category, Native_State, Workshops_Attended_Count) in a logistic model with Workshop_Batch_Number as a control.
  • Investigate batch heterogeneity, especially batch 14 (+23.57 pp) vs batches 11 and 17. Look at facilitator, venue, cohort-size and survey-administration differences, and treat per-batch gaps with caution given small strata.
  • Audit the zero-coded Metric_Life_Satisfaction_Global values, especially in batch 17 (usable n falls from 174 to 35). Confirm whether the item was skipped or not administered. Consider reporting life satisfaction only for batches with adequate coverage.
  • Make an explicit decision on 'Not yet decided / confused'. This analysis counts it as non-full-time, but the schema treats it as null for ordinal models. Report a sensitivity analysis excluding it, and note the undecided stratum shows the largest gender gap (54.43% vs 42.67%).
  • Because conversion into full-time, not general upward movement, drives the female edge, follow up with demographics.Current_Status_Of_Work (using the label_map: 'Social Impact Work' positive, 'Previously SIW' engaged-then-left) to see whether stated Post intentions translate into later outcomes. Avoid leaking Post-phase columns into any long-term model, and control for batch censoring.
  • Consider complementing the 1–5 mean-delta with ordinal and share-based metrics (share ≥2-point gain, Cliff's delta), because the female advantage is concentrated in larger gains (37.98% vs 32.45%) while medians are equal (1). Where a single test is preferred, use a non-parametric one given the discrete delta.
